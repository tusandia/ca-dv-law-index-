Coding scheme: California laws and DV survivors

Unit of analysis: one bill (latest version), or one code section. Code every item from its title, Legislative Counsel's Digest and, if still unclear, the bill text. Code what the law does, not what its author says it is for.

1. relevance (pick one)

Code Rule Examples
direct The law does at least one of these: (a) names DV with a core term, meaning one the reference sources use as a name for DV (domestic violence, domestic abuse, intimate partner violence, dating violence, family violence); (b) cites or uses a statutory DV term: the Domestic Violence Prevention Act (Fam. Code §6200 et seq.), Fam. Code §6203/§6211, Pen. Code §273.5, Evid. Code §1037, coercive control, forced marriage, emergency protective orders, or a federal DV law or program (VAWA, military protective orders); (c) describes abuse between people in a statutory relationship (cohabitants, household members, former spouses, dating partners, fiancés), the way California statutes define DV without saying "domestic violence"; or (d) uses two or more supporting terms of abuse together, such as "batterer" with "protective order," or "emotional abuse" with "power and control." DVRO procedure; DV shelter funding; coercive control as abuse; a new crime of battery against a cohabitant.
indirect The law does not need to mention DV, but it materially changes what a survivor can get or what risks they face in at least one impact area below, and survivors are a group it clearly reaches more than incidentally. Expanding eligibility for U-visa certifications; lease termination rights for crime victims; firearm relinquishment procedures; court interpreter access in family court; address confidentiality; services offered regardless of immigration status; rules on service of process or ex parte divorce; jurisdiction in custody cases that cross state or national borders; funding for culturally specific organizations.
none Neither. A passing mention of "victims" in a broad bill does not make it indirect unless the change would matter to a survivor's safety, housing, status, income, children or access to courts.

Tie-breakers:

  • Bills covering several crimes (e.g. "victims of domestic violence, sexual assault, stalking or human trafficking") that name DV are direct.
  • Elder / dependent adult abuse and child abuse alone are indirect only if they apply within a family or household relationship that would meet Fam. Code §6211; otherwise none.
  • Spot bills or bills whose text is only intent language: code what the digest says the bill would do; add spot bill in notes.
  • Abuse by in-laws or other family members counts as DV when the relationship meets Fam. Code §6211, which covers more than spouses. Transnational marriage abandonment is usually carried out by the husband and his family (Bhandari 2025).
  • A law can hurt survivors. Code it direct or indirect all the same, and say in notes that the effect is harmful. Examples: a law that makes interpreters harder to get, or a law that lets an abuser use a protective order or a false abuse claim against the survivor.

When coding by hand, apply the same four routes. A bill that only says "victim" or "abuse" is never direct: those words are common in DV writing, but also appear in about 3% of all bills, mostly about crime victims in general, child abuse or substance use.

How terms are weighted

The tool's weights (lexicon.csv, column weight) come from four reference sources, measured by source_weights.py and set by reweight.py. Every term's evidence is recorded in the src_* columns.

  • Breadth: how many of the four sources use the term.
  • Specificity: the weighted log-odds z-score of the term in the sources against all 4,262 bill summaries (Monroe, Colaresi & Quinn 2008), plus the share of bills that use it.
Weight Direct terms Victim/safety terms (anchors)
3 Core names for DV (in 2+ sources); statutory terms; relationship + abuse (none)
2 Supporting terms used by 3 or 4 sources Used by all 4 sources and in under 2% of bills
1 Supporting terms used by 0–2 sources Everything else, including any word in 2%+ of bills

Decision rule for the tool: direct = the summed weights of distinct direct terms reach 3. That happens with one weight-3 term, or with two or more supporting terms. Indirect = an impact-area term plus anchor weight of 2 or more, or a strong impact-area term on its own.

Three cautions, which belong in the write-up:

  1. Advocacy and statutory words differ (Lovell 2025). "Survivor," "abuser," "batterer" and "power and control" are common in the sources but rare in bills: "power and control" appears in no 2025–26 bill summary. Statutory terms and code citations appear in no source but define DV in California law. That's why statutory terms get weight 3 regardless of source counts.
  2. One source can dominate. Lovell's article discusses terms ("experiencer" appears 121 times, all in his article), so anchors need all four sources before they get extra weight.
  3. Forms of abuse from other sources lose weight. Dowry, transnational abandonment and immigration-related abuse (Bhandari 2025; Tabibi et al. 2018) aren't in these four sources, so they now count 1 each, as supporting terms. They no longer make a bill direct on their own, but they still count toward route (d).

Effect on 2025–26 (summaries only): direct stays 59 (54 by core name, 5 by statutory term); indirect goes from 73 to 46, mostly bills whose only signal was "victim."

2b. abuse_forms (any the law responds to, separated by ;)

The kinds of abuse the law addresses. A law about restraining orders in general gets general. The list follows the Learning Network's typology (Tabibi et al. 2018) plus two forms documented among South Asian women in the U.S. (Bhandari 2025).

Code Covers
general Abuse in general, with no form singled out.
physical Hitting, choking or strangulation, withholding hormones or medication.
sexual Sexual assault, reproductive coercion, forced abortion, refusal to use protection.
psychological Isolation, threats, coercive control, threats to "out" a partner, harm to pets or service animals.
economic Control of money or accounts, coerced debt, dowry demands, keeping a partner from working.
technology Monitoring, tracking, stalkerware, intimate images, abuse through devices and social media.
neglect Withholding food, care, medication or mobility aids.
forced_marriage Forcing someone into marriage, or keeping them in one.
immigration_related Threats about status or deportation, withholding or destroying passports and visas, refusing to file or withdrawing a sponsorship, keeping a partner from learning English.
transnational_abandonment Leaving a spouse stranded abroad, cancelling her visa or tickets, locking her out after migration, serving divorce ex parte in another country (Bhandari 2025).
child_related Using children to control: abduction, alienation, false abuse claims, fights over custody jurisdiction.
faith_misuse Using religion or a faith leader to justify abuse or keep a survivor from leaving (Tabibi et al. 2018).

3. populations (any that apply)

Survivors the law specifically reaches. Tag a realm only when the law itself reaches that group; don't infer from who tends to use a program.

Code Covers
immigrant_vawa Immigration status, VAWA self-petitions, U/T visas, asylum, refugees, noncitizens, public charge.
women_gender Women, gender, gender-based violence, pregnancy, reproductive health, forced or coerced marriage.
faith Religion, places of worship, clergy, faith-based organizations, but only where faith changes something for survivors. Examples: shelters or services that meet religious and spiritual needs, religious discrimination in victim services, immigration enforcement at places of worship, and the role of faith leaders, who can help a survivor or side with the abuser (Bhandari 2025). A passing mention in a list of protected traits doesn't count.
language_access Interpreters, translation, limited English proficiency. In notes, name any language the law lists.
military Service members, veterans and their partners; military protective orders.
youth, elder_dependent_adult, disability, tribal, lgbtq, rural As named.

No 2025–26 bill names a religion, ethnicity or South Asian language. Muslim and South Asian survivors are therefore not separate codes. Their concerns are tracked through the realms above and through abuse_forms.

Caution against "culturalizing" violence. Code what the law does, not assumptions about a community. Violence against women happens in every culture. Treating it as a product of one community's culture, rather than of structural inequality, is itself a harm (Tabibi et al. 2018). A forced-marriage law is women_gender and forced_marriage. It isn't tagged to any religion or ethnicity. This is how the coding handles the main risk named in the proposal: indirect laws that matter mostly to a specific group.

4. notes

One line: why it is indirect (what changes for survivors), or why a close call went to none. These notes are what you'll use to revise the lexicon.

Procedure

  1. Code blind: use coding/coding_sheet.csv only, never sample_key.csv.

  2. After two weeks, re-code a random 15% of rows in a fresh copy of the sheet and run evaluate.py --recode for intra-coder agreement (Cohen's kappa).

  3. Keep a log of rule changes; if a rule changes, re-check rows already coded.

  4. Outside check: python legiscan_check.py runs LegiScan's full-text search for each codebook concept and lists bills it finds that the tool did not flag. Code those bills by these same rules. A relevant one means a missing term or rule: add it to lexicon.csv and note it in the change log. LegiScan is a second keyword system, not a gold standard, so report it next to the hand-coded evaluation, not instead of it.

The federal layer

Federal law sets a floor that California builds on, and many state bills only make sense next to it. reference/federal_statutes_womenslaw.csv lists the 71 federal statutes and regulations on WomensLaw's Selected Federal Statutes page. Each is marked dv_specific:

  • yes (12): about DV, e.g. VAWA housing (34 U.S.C. §12491), VAWA confidentiality (8 U.S.C. §1367), UCMJ Art. 128b.

  • partly (24): a general law with a DV provision, e.g. the gun bans in 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(8)–(9), U visas, VAWA self-petitions.

  • no (35): listed for context only, e.g. asylum filing, T visas, penalties.

Federal statutes are not coded in the index. Code a California bill against them like this:

  • A bill that adopts, extends or fills a gap in a federal protection is indirect at least. Examples: VAWA-style housing rights for state-funded or private housing, which VAWA itself doesn't cover; U-visa certification rules (Pen. Code §679.10); recognition of military or tribal protection orders. Note the federal law in notes.

  • A bill on military survivors: tag the population military. Military protective orders, the Family Advocacy Program and restricted reporting are federal, so state bills mostly concern recognizing and enforcing MPOs (e.g. SB 99).

  • A bill that changes state custody jurisdiction (Fam. Code §3400 et seq., the UCCJEA): custody, with child_related in abuse_forms.

Federal terms are in lexicon.csv (Section 8 vouchers, lease bifurcation, emergency transfers, self-petitions, U/T nonimmigrant, military protective orders, full faith and credit). In 2025–26 only two bills use any of them (AB 1753 on foreign protection orders, SB 99 on military protective orders). Both were already flagged.

What the index can't capture

  • Federal law and policy (e.g. expedited removal, enforcement priorities, federal immigration relief). These change what Californians can use but never show up as California bills; see the federal layer above for context.

  • County and city ordinances, court rules (Judicial Council), and agency regulations.

  • Laws whose effect on survivors depends on implementation or funding, not text.

  • Bills where DV relevance is only clear from committee analyses or advocates' positions, not from the digest or text.

  • Problems that cross borders. In transnational abandonment, the husband files in one country while the wife is in another, so no single state's law reaches the whole case (Bhandari 2025). Remedies sit mostly in federal immigration law (VAWA self-petitions, work permits for dependent-visa holders such as H-4) and in other countries' laws.

  • Gaps. An index of what passed can't show what was never introduced. The 2025–26 session has no bill that mentions dowry, transnational or marriage abandonment, immigration-related abuse, international child abduction or the Hague Convention, culturally specific services, or faith leaders. Report these absences as findings.
